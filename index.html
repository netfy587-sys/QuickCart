REV. 03 — FOOTWEAR
FOOTWEAR / FIELD GUIDE

The one piece of kit that actually touches the ground.

Every other layer sits on your body. Your shoes sit between you and the surface, absorbing thousands of impacts a run. Here's how to actually choose, break in, and retire a pair — without the marketing noise.

01 — STRUCTURE

What's actually inside a running shoe

Four parts do almost all the work. Knowing what each one does makes it much easier to tell a genuine upgrade from a colourway change.

  • A

    Upper

    The woven or knit material wrapping the foot — controls breathability and how the shoe holds the foot in place.

  • B

    Midsole

    The foam layer doing the actual cushioning and energy return. This is what "soft" or "responsive" really refers to.

  • C

    Outsole

    Rubber underneath that grips the surface. Tread pattern changes by terrain — road, track, and trail all need different grip.

  • D

    Heel counter

    The firm cup around the heel. It's what stops your foot sliding inside the shoe on turns and downhills.

02 — FIT

Matching a shoe to how you actually stand

Arch type is a rough starting filter, not a strict rule — but it narrows a wall of options down to a shortlist worth trying on.

High arch

Foot rolls outward on landing (under-pronation). Needs cushioning that absorbs shock rather than fighting the motion.

LOOK FOR: soft, flexible midsole

Neutral arch

Foot lands and rolls inward slightly, evenly. The widest category — most standard cushioned trainers are built around this.

LOOK FOR: balanced, all-purpose cushioning

Flat / low arch

Foot rolls inward more than average (over-pronation). Often benefits from added structure along the inner edge of the midsole.

LOOK FOR: stability or support features

A wet-foot test on paper or a specialty running-store gait check will tell you more than guessing — this is a starting point, not a diagnosis.

03 — SURFACE

Road, track, and trail want different soles

The same shoe rarely does all three well. Outsole grip and midsole firmness both shift depending on where you're actually running.

ROAD
Smooth, consistent surface

Flat outsole tread, more midsole cushioning for repetitive impact on hard pavement.

TRACK
Rubberised, springy surface

Lighter builds with minimal tread — some runners use flats or spikes here for speed sessions.

TRAIL
Uneven, loose surface

Deep lugs for grip, a lower profile for stability, and a tougher upper to handle roots and rock.

05 — LAST DETAIL

Two lacing fixes worth knowing

Before buying a different size, a different lacing pattern often solves the exact same complaint.

Heel slipping? Try a heel-lock lace

Cross the laces through the extra top eyelets to form a loop on each side, then thread the opposite lace through before tying — it locks the heel in place without over-tightening the forefoot.

Top-of-foot pressure? Skip an eyelet

Lacing straight up past the eyelet over the sore spot, instead of crossing through it, relieves pressure without loosening the rest of the fit.

Toes going numb? Loosen the forefoot

The bottom two or three eyelets can sit looser than the rest — most of the hold you need comes from the mid and upper laces, not the front.

06 — UPKEEP

Keeping a pair honest

Small habits that keep a shoe performing closer to its first-run feel for longer.

01
Air-dry, never direct heat

Radiators and dryers warp midsole foam and weaken adhesives over time.

02
Remove insoles to dry fully

Trapped moisture under the insole is where odour and breakdown both start.

03
Loosen laces before removing

Forcing a foot out of a tied shoe collapses the heel counter over time.

04
Track mileage, not months

A shoe run twice a week ages very differently to one run daily — log distance, not calendar time.

BEFORE YOU BUY

Three questions worth asking in the shop

No pair is objectively "the best" — only better or worse matched to how and where you actually run.
